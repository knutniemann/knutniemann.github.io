Political Distance and Trade

Political distance constrained trade most strongly in the late Cold War. Applying that responsiveness to the 2020 economy raises the estimated welfare cost of political realignment.

Using UN voting disagreement and a structural gravity model for 167 countries over 1966–2020, I find that a typical within-pair increase in political distance is associated with 3 percent lower trade. The relationship weakens after the Cold War and subsequently recovers only partially. Under a trade-cost interpretation, applying late-Cold-War rather than recent responsiveness to the same political realignment raises the average welfare loss by about 40 percent.

Abstract

Amid geopolitical tensions, I quantify how much political distance reduces trade and whether it depends on the geopolitical environment. Measuring political distance by UN General Assembly voting disagreement, I estimate a structural gravity model for 167 countries over 1966–2020. A one-standard-deviation increase in political distance within a country pair is associated with 3 percent lower trade, a quarter of a regional trade agreement's effect. This response is delayed rather than anticipatory, consistent with a causal interpretation. The responsiveness peaked in the late Cold War, was weak and insignificant during 1995–2007, and strengthened again by 2016–2020 without reaching its peak. Under a trade-cost interpretation, I assess the welfare effects of continued political realignment by imposing 1962 political distances in 2020. This brings some countries closer and pushes others apart. With 2016–2020 trade responsiveness, average welfare is half a percent lower; all else equal, late-Cold-War responsiveness increases the loss by almost half.

Firm Expectations and News: Micro v Macro

with Benjamin Born, Zeno Enders, Manuel Menkhoff, and Gernot J. Müller

German and Italian firms overreact to firm-specific news but underreact to aggregate news. A model of “island illusion” explains these patterns and traces their aggregate implications.

Abstract

To shed light on the expectation formation of firms, we use firm-level data and examine how expectations adjust to news. We classify news as either micro (firm-specific developments) or macro (information about the aggregate economy). Analyzing survey data from Italian and German firms, we consistently find that expectations overreact to micro news but underreact to macro news. Our empirical specification is based on a stylized model of ‘island illusion,’ because of which firms systematically underestimate the importance of aggregate developments for their own performance. This illusion has aggregate implications, which we derive by embedding it into the canonical noisy-information model.

Political Shocks, Interstate Migration, and Geographic Polarization

with Jonas Adolph

Close US gubernatorial elections increase outmigration from losing-party counties and redirect moves towards the losing party's strongholds. Event studies and a migration-selection model connect these responses to geographic political sorting.

Abstract

Geographic political sorting has intensified in the United States, raising the question whether political events shape internal migration in ways that reinforce it. Combining annual county-to-county Internal Revenue Service migration flows with 45 U.S. gubernatorial elections decided by less than five percentage points between 1994 and 2014, we compare locally losing and winning counties in stacked event studies. During the first five post-election years, outmigration from losing counties rises by 3.21 percent relative to winning counties. Conditional on total departures and destination shocks, migration from losing counties shifts by 4.02 percent toward strongholds of the losing party relative to winner-party strongholds. The increase in outmigration is concentrated in races with large candidate-ideology gaps, consistent with larger political shocks moving more residents across the migration threshold. A migration-selection model rationalizes both margins, while gravity estimates showing less migration between politically distant states provide corroborating evidence. Together, the results show that political events can redirect internal migration in a direction consistent with greater geographic sorting.

Tariffs and Danish Exports: Insights from Three Decades of Microdata

with Henrik Yde Andersen, Mathias Klein, and Thomas Krause

Using three decades of Danish microdata, we study exporters' responses to foreign tariffs. Preliminary evidence suggests declines in export quantities and unit values, consistent with exporters absorbing part of the tariff increase.

Project details

How do exporters in a small open economy respond to foreign tariff changes? We link Danish customs records, firm balance sheets, and destination-specific tariffs from 1993 to 2021. Comparing the same firm's exports of a product across destinations, we use local projections to trace adjustments in export revenues, quantities, and unit values. Preliminary results suggest that higher foreign tariffs reduce both quantities and export unit values, consistent with exporters absorbing part of the tariff increase. We also examine employment, investment, and destination diversification, with initial evidence pointing to limited domestic adjustment and possible expansion into additional markets.
